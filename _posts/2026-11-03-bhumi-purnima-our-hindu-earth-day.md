# **Bhūmi Pūrṇimā: Our Hindu Earth Day**

### *An ancient tradition of thanking, protecting, and celebrating Mother Earth*

On **Āśvayuja Pūrṇimā**, the full-moon day that comes after **Navarātri and Vijayadaśamī** in the Hindu month of Āśvayuja or Āśvina, farming families in the sacred **Sahyādri mountain region of India** gather in their fields. They decorate the land, perform pūjā to **Bhūmi Devi and Śrī Lakṣmī**, offer food, and share a festive meal among the ripening crops. They eat together in the fields and share food with **family members, children, neighbors, and those who work upon the land**.

This day is observed in almost all places to various degrees and way where the Sanatan practitioners live. In Karnataka, this celebration is called **Bhūmi Hunnime** or **Sīge Hunnime**. In Kannada, *hunnime* means the full-moon day. **Bhūmi Hunnime therefore means Bhūmi Pūrṇimā, “the Full Moon of the Earth.”** It is a beautiful Hindu Earth Day, rooted not in fear of nature, but in gratitude to the mother who sustains every form of life. This ancient tradition of honouring the mother Earth is celebrated throughout India, Srilanka, Nepal, Tibet, Thailand, Laos, Myanmar by doing puja to earth or following comparable practices. Even in the traditions of the ancient Greek, Anglo-Saxon, Celtic, Slavic cultures remarkably similar festivals and rituals are found on this same Sharad fullmoon day.

## **How Is Bhūmi Pūrṇimā Celebrated?**

Bhūmi Pūrṇimā comes when the monsoon-fed crops are growing toward maturity. The green field, filled with developing grain, is treated like a mother carrying new life.

Traditionally, families:

* clean and decorate a small place in the field  
* perform pūjā to Bhūmi Devi and the standing crops  
* offer turmeric, kumkum, flowers, fruits, milk, grains, and festive foods  
* carry baskets containing dishes such as *kadubu*, *holige*, *chitrānna*, vegetables, and seasonal greens  
* offer a small quantity of food, traditionally called **charaga**, to the field  
* share a meal with family members, neighbors, and those who work upon the land  
* visit neighboring fields and join one another’s celebrations  
* refrain from digging, drilling, plowing, or otherwise disturbing the soil that day

One beautiful traditional explanation compares the celebration to a **sīmantha**, the Hindu ceremony that blesses an expectant mother. At this time of year, the field is carrying the community’s future food. Bhūmi Devi is therefore lovingly decorated, offered festive foods, and thanked like a mother carrying new life.

The precise customs vary by family and place. The heart of the celebration remains the same:

> **The Earth that feeds us must be thanked, respected, and allowed to rest.**

Bhūmi Pūrṇimā belongs to a wider seasonal culture found wherever Sanātana traditions have flourished and, through related ideas and practices, beyond. In Maharashtra, the Āśvayuja season includes **Bhondla**, also known regionally as **Hadga** or **Bhulabai**. Families celebrate with traditional songs, an elephant image, circular movement, and shared festive food. The observance is associated with rainfall, the agricultural season, prosperity, and community well-being. In some communities, it continues until **Kojāgiri Pūrṇimā**, the same full-moon tithi on which Bhūmi Pūrṇimā is observed. Pūjā and reverence for Mother Earth are not limited to one region of India. They are found across Bhārat, throughout neighboring regions: **Śarad Pūrṇimā or Kaumudī Mahotsava** across northern and western India; **Rāsa Pūrṇimā** in Vaiṣṇava traditions; **Kumāra Pūrṇimā** in Odisha; **Kojāgarī Lakṣmī Pūjā** in Bengal and Assam; new-grain observances known in some regions as **Navānna**; and **Kojāgrat Pūrṇimā** in Nepal.

## **Why Is This a Hindu Earth Day?**

Modern environmental observances encourage us to protect the planet. Bhūmi Pūrṇimā teaches this responsibility through a distinctly Hindu relationship:

> **Earth is not merely a resource. Earth is our mother.**

Sanātana Dharma calls her by many sacred names:

* **Bhūmi**, the ground that supports us  
* **Pṛthivī**, the vast and expansive one  
* **Dharā or Dharaṇī**, she who bears and sustains  
* **Vasundharā**, bearer of riches  
* **Bhū Devi**, the divine Mother Earth

When we see the Earth as our mother, environmental responsibility becomes more than a rule. It becomes **dharma**.

## **The Vedic Voice of Mother Earth**

The best-known scriptural foundation for this Hindu understanding is the **Bhūmi Sūkta**, also called the **Pṛthivī Sūkta**, found in the *Atharva Veda* 12.1.

This great hymn praises the Earth as the bearer of mountains, rivers, plants, medicinal herbs, animals, farms, food, communities, and human civilization. It recognizes that human beings depend upon a vast living order that they did not create.

Its most memorable declaration is:

> **माता भूमिः पुत्रोऽहं पृथिव्याः ।**  
>  *Mātā bhūmiḥ putro’haṁ pṛthivyāḥ.*  
>  **“Earth is my mother, and I am her child.”**  
>  *Atharva Veda* 12.1.12

The mantra also invokes **Parjanya**, the life-giving rain, as father. Earth, rain, seed, plant, animal, and human life are thus understood as members of one sacred and interdependent family.

Another remarkable mantra says:

> **“Whatever of you I dig, O Earth, may that grow back quickly.**  
>  **May I not injure your vital places or your heart.”**  
>  *Atharva Veda* 12.1.35

This is more than praise of nature. It expresses an ethic of restraint:

1. Take only what is needed.  
2. Help the Earth regenerate.  
3. Do not damage the sources that sustain life.

The *Atharva Veda* does not prescribe the particular regional festival called Bhūmi Hunnime. What it clearly establishes is something even more fundamental:

> **Reverence for Earth and responsibility toward her are ancient Vedic values.**

## **How Old Is This Understanding?**

Earth, water, rainfall, plants, animals, fertility, and food have been sacred throughout the long history of Indian civilization. Archaeological remains from the **Indus-Sarasvatī civilization** include representations of animals, vegetation, water, fertility, and regeneration. Terracotta figurines of a mother figure with plant foliage issuing from her womb (specifically the seals showing a plant growing from a woman's body) have been found. **Neolithic/Megalithic Stone Sites have yielded** rural field practices of worshipping *Pancha Kallu* \- five unshaped stones set in fields representing soil deities).  It is clear from these in the Indus-Sarasvatī and even before there were ideas and probably celebration of and puja of Earth. What these remains do demonstrate is the deep importance of water, agriculture, animals, and seasonal abundance in early Indian/Hindu civilization.

The Vedic hymns give this relationship an explicit sacred voice. Later Hindu traditions honor the Earth as **Bhū Devi**, remember her in the narratives of **Varāha** and **King Pṛthu**, seek her permission before disturbing the ground through **Bhūmi Pūjā**, and ask her forgiveness before placing our feet upon her.

Bhūmi Pūrṇimā belongs within this long and living Sanātana relationship with Mother Earth.

## **Mother Earth Across the Hindu Calendar**

Bhūmi Pūrṇimā is a particularly direct celebration of Mother Earth, but Hindu communities honor the Earth and the natural order on many occasions.

| Observance | Region and season | How Mother Earth and nature are honored |
| :---: | ----- | ----- |
| **Bhūmi Hunnime or Sīge Hunnime** | Karnataka and the Sahyādri mountain region, on Āśvayuja Pūrṇimā | Families perform pūjā to Bhūmi Tāyi and the ripening crops, offer festive food, visit neighboring fields, eat together, and avoid disturbing the soil. The field may be lovingly honored like an expectant mother receiving *sīmantha*. |
| **Bhondla, Hadga, or Bhulabai** | Maharashtra, during Āśvayuja, sometimes continuing until Kojāgiri Pūrṇimā | Traditional songs, an elephant image, circular movement, and shared food celebrate rainfall, seasonal abundance, agricultural well-being, and community. |
| **Raja Parba** | Odisha, near the beginning of the monsoon | Mother Earth is associated with fertility, rest, and renewal. Activities that disturb the soil are paused, and the season is welcomed with festive foods, songs, swings, and community celebrations. |
| **Ambubachi** | Kāmākhyā in Assam, during the monsoon | The Divine Mother’s annual period of fertility and regeneration is observed through sacred rest and the reopening of the temple after ritual purification. |
| **Pongal and Makara Saṅkrānti** | Across Bhārat, during the harvest season | Families express gratitude to Sūrya, Mother Earth, rainfall, cattle, farmers, and everyone whose work brings food to the community. |
| **Bhūmi Pūjā** | Before construction or cultivation | Permission and blessings are sought from Mother Earth before the ground is disturbed. |
| **Daily Bhūmi Vandana** | Practiced in Hindu homes | Mother Earth’s forgiveness is sought before touching her with one’s feet. |

Together, these traditions teach that nature has rhythms of rainfall, growth, fertility, harvest, rest, and renewal. Human beings must respect those rhythms.

## **The Daily Prayer to Mother Earth**

Environmental responsibility in Sanātana Dharma does not begin and end with an annual festival. It can begin every morning. Many Hindu children learn to seek Mother Earth’s forgiveness before placing their feet upon the floor:

> **समुद्रवसने देवि पर्वतस्तनमण्डले ।**  
>  **विष्णुपत्नि नमस्तुभ्यं पादस्पर्शं क्षमस्व मे ॥**

> *Samudra-vasane Devi, parvata-stana-maṇḍale,*  
>  *Viṣṇu-patni namastubhyaṁ, pāda-sparśaṁ kṣamasva me.*

> “O Devi, clothed by the oceans and adorned with mountains,  
>  O consort of Viṣṇu, I bow to you.  
>  Please forgive the touch of my feet.”

Even before beginning the day, a Hindu child is taught: **Walk upon the Earth with gratitude and humility.**

# **A Simple Bhūmi Pūrṇimā Celebration for Families**

Families in cities do not need a farm or an elaborate ceremony. A backyard, balcony, community garden, temple garden, local park, or clean indoor space can become the setting.

## **1\. Create a Place for Bhūmi Devi**

You can decorate your garden in the backyard or your decorative plant pots at home or place a small bowl or pot containing clean soil on a tray. Children may decorate it with flowers, leaves, turmeric, and a rice-flour rangoli.

## **2\. Perform a Simple Pūjā**

Recite shlokas you know and offer water, flowers, fruits, grains, and a small portion of the family meal.

## **3\. Recite the Vedic Teaching**

The whole family may say together:

> **Mātā bhūmiḥ putro’haṁ pṛthivyāḥ: Earth is my mother, and I am her child.**

Ask each child to name one gift received from Earth, such as water, food, trees, medicine, clothing, shelter, or clean air.

## **4\. Give the Soil a Day of Rest**

Do not dig, mow, or use power tools on the soil that day. Explain to children that the pause is a traditional act of gratitude. It teaches us that we do not need to alter, consume, or control nature every day.

## **5\. Share a Meal Outdoors**

If the weather permits, eat outdoors as a family. Invite friends or neighbors to join.

## **6\. Perform One Act of Bhūmi Sevā**

Choose something practical:

* plant a native tree or pollinator-friendly flower  
* remove litter from a park or riverbank  
* begin composting suitable food scraps  
* reduce disposable plastic at home  
* repair or reuse something instead of discarding it  
* support a local farm or community garden  
* learn the names of five local plants  
* conserve water  
* avoid chemicals that can harm water, bees, or butterflies

## **A Question for Children**

Ask your child:

> **“If Earth is truly my mother, what will I do differently today?”**

A young child may promise not to litter. An older child may conserve water, reduce waste, begin composting, learn about native plants, or help protect a local habitat.

The action can be small. What matters is developing the right relationship.

# **Our Earth Day and Our Daily Dharma**

Earth Day is internationally observed on **April 22**, when Hindu children can participate wholeheartedly in school cleanups, tree planting, recycling programs, and community environmental projects in the spirit of **Bhūmi Sevā**. Our traditional Hindu Earth Day is Bhūmi Pūrṇimā, observed on Āśvayuja Pūrṇimā after Navarātri. Its values of gratitude, restraint, conservation, and service are to be practiced every day.

Throughout the year, we practice its values through gratitude, restraint, conservation, and service.

> **Earth is our mother. We are her children. Her care is our daily dharma.**

## **Bhūmi Pūrṇimā in 30 Seconds**

* **What is it?** A Hindu celebration of Mother Earth and the maturing crops.  
* **When is it?** Āśvayuja or Āśvina Pūrṇimā (Fullmoon day after Navaratri).  
* **Where is it celebrated as Bhūmi Purnima?** Explicitly and so in parts of the sacred Sahyādri mountain region but throughout the ancient world.  
* **What does the name mean?** “The Full Moon of the Earth.”  
* **What is the wider regional connection?** Maharashtra’s Bhondla or Hadga celebrates the same Āśvayuja agricultural season and, in some communities, continues until Kojāgiri Pūrṇimā.  
* **What is its Vedic foundation?** The *Atharva Veda* praises Earth as our mother and teaches restraint when using her resources.  
* **How can children celebrate?** Perform a simple pūjā, offer gratitude, let the soil rest, share a family meal, avoid waste, and perform one act of Bhūmi Sevā.  
* **What is the essential mantra?**

> **Mātā bhūmiḥ putro’haṁ pṛthivyāḥ.**  
>  Earth is my mother, and I am her child.

> “Earth is my mother, and I am her child,” declares the Atharva Veda. This Āśvayuja Pūrṇimā, celebrate Bhūmi Pūrṇimā through pūjā, gratitude, rest for the soil, a shared family meal, and one meaningful act of Bhūmi Sevā.

## **A Wider Tradition of Gratitude to Mother Earth**

**Pūjā and reverence for Mother Earth are not limited to one region of India. They are found across Bhārat, throughout neighboring regions, and among many of the world’s ancient traditions.** Their customs and calendars vary, but they express a shared understanding: human life depends upon the Earth, waters, rainfall, fertility, and the recurring seasons.

Across **Bhārata Khaṇḍa**, the full moon of Āśvayuja or Āśvina is celebrated in many regional forms. It is observed as **Bhūmi Hunnime or Sīge Hunnime** in parts of Karnataka; **Kojāgiri Pūrṇimā** in Maharashtra; **Śarad Pūrṇimā or Kaumudī Mahotsava** across northern and western India; **Rāsa Pūrṇimā** in Vaiṣṇava traditions; **Kumāra Pūrṇimā** in Odisha; **Kojāgarī Lakṣmī Pūjā** in Bengal and Assam; new-grain observances known in some regions as **Navānna**; and **Kojāgrat Pūrṇimā** in Nepal.

The customs include Bhūmi Pūjā, Lakṣmī Pūjā, Chandra Pūjā, Rāsa celebrations, devotional singing and dancing, offerings of newly ripened grain, *kheer* or sweetened milk placed beneath the autumn moon, and community feasts.

In Maharashtra, **Bhondla**, also called **Hadga** or **Bhulabai**, is celebrated during the same Āśvayuja season. Participants gather around an elephant image, sing traditional songs, move in a circle, and share *khirāpat*. In some communities, the observance continues until Kojāgiri Pūrṇimā. 

The same sacred full moon is significant across neighboring regions. In **Sri Lanka**, it is observed as **Vap Poya** and is associated with **Vap Magula**, the traditional royal plowing ceremony in which kings and national leaders ceremonially begin the agricultural season.

In **Thailand**, **Wan Ok Phansa** marks the completion of the rain’s retreat. In parts of the country, **Lai Ruea Fai** fills rivers with illuminated boats made from bamboo and banana trunks, carrying lamps, flowers, and offerings. The celebration expresses gratitude and releases misfortune through light offered upon the waters. 

In **Myanmar**, the three-day **Thadingyut Festival of Lights** commemorates Bhagavān Buddha’s return to Earth after teaching in the celestial realm. Homes, streets, and sacred places are illuminated, and gratitude is expressed to parents, teachers, and elders.

In **Laos**, **Boun Ok Phansa** is observed with lamps, river processions, and floating offerings at the conclusion of the rain’s retreat. In **Cambodia**, the same post-monsoon period leads into the **Kathina** season of offerings. Though the ceremonies vary, their timing remains connected with the completion of the rains, rivers, cultivation, travel, seasonal renewal, and community life.

Seasonal gatherings connected with ripening fields, new grain, household prosperity, rivers, and rainfall have also been found across **Punjab, Sindh, Afghanistan, Tibet, Iran, and Central Asia**, although their precise dates and customs differ.

Among some **Sindhi Hindu** families, Śarad or Kojāgiri Pūrṇimā traditions include gratitude to **Varuṇa Deva**, Śrī Lakṣmī, rivers, water, and the sources of household prosperity.

The ancient Persian festival of **Mehregān**, although not fixed to the Hindu lunar tithi of Āśvayuja Pūrṇimā, similarly celebrated the autumn season and agricultural abundance. Families gathered for festive meals with fruits, seeds, grains, breads, flowers, and other symbols of prosperity.

Comparable traditions also appeared in ancient Europe. The Greek **Thesmophoria** honored **Demeter and Persephone** during the agricultural season. Its observances included fasting, sitting upon the bare Earth, and prayers for the fertility of crops and the continuity of life.

Slavic traditions revered **Mat Zemlya**, literally “Mother Earth,” and preserved seasonal restrictions against unnecessarily striking, digging, or disturbing the soil. These customs expressed respect for the land’s fertility and its period of winter rest.

Celtic communities marked the completion of the harvest with feasts, offerings, and remembrance of the land’s abundance. Figures such as **Tailtiu** and **Anu** became associated in different traditions with the land, fertility, nourishment, and sovereignty.

Germanic and Scandinavian traditions honored the Earth, the ancestral mothers, and female guardian beings through observances associated with the **Dísir**, **Jörð**, and the beginning of winter. **Winter Nights**, or *Vetrnætr*, marked the seasonal transition and included offerings for protection, fertility, and future abundance. **Mōdraniht**, or “Mothers’ Night,” was a related celebration of ancestral mothers, although it belonged to the winter-solstice season rather than the autumn full moon.

Some occur on Āśvayuja Pūrṇimā, while others belong to the wider autumn harvest and seasonal cycle. Yet they preserve a common civilizational insight:

> **Human beings depend upon the Earth. Her gifts must be received with gratitude, and earth must not be treated carelessly.**

Sanātana Dharma expresses this relationship with exceptional clarity:

> **माता भूमिः पुत्रोऽहं पृथिव्याः ।**  
>  *Mātā bhūmiḥ putro’haṁ pṛthivyāḥ.*  
>  **“Earth is my mother, and I am her child.”**  
>  *Atharva Veda* 12.1.12

Bhūmi Pūrṇimā therefore belongs to a distinct and living Sanātana tradition whose ideas influenced the ancient world and preserved an enduring human recognition: that the Earth, water, harvest, and seasonal order are sacred trusts deserving gratitude and care.
